How long does it take a property sourcer to find a deal?

A useful estimate starts with your brief. Here is how to think about timing, the work behind a deal and your first steps with Kestrel.

By Kestrel Sourcing18 October 20265 min read
Start here

Before putting a date in your diary, agree what that date means: receiving an opportunity to assess, having an offer accepted, or completing the purchase. Each is a different milestone, with different work still to do.

What do published timescales suggest?

PropSourcer’s guide gives the following indicative ranges. Its sourcing estimates start from agreeing terms; completion is a separate stage.

Indicative ranges from PropSourcer’s guide
Stage or strategyTime suggested
General sourcing and packaging4–12 weeks
Commercial property sourcing8–16 weeks
Land and development site sourcing8 weeks to 6 months or more
Conveyancing and completionA further 8–16 weeks

Source: PropSourcer’s property sourcing timescales guide, updated 7 July 2026. These are the publisher’s estimates, not independently verified averages or Kestrel delivery commitments. They should not be treated as a forecast for a particular purchase.

What happens first at Kestrel?

Our starting point is understanding what you want to achieve. Kestrel’s current enquiry process gives you a discovery call or a written discovery form, followed by a personalised consultation.

The sourcing agreement comes next. Proof of funds and ID are requested after the consultation and signed agreement. These steps establish the basis for the relationship before a search proceeds under the agreed terms.

You can begin through Join Our Investor Network. At this stage, explain the project you are looking for and where you need help. You do not need to arrive with every answer settled.

Put a decision rule behind your brief

A useful brief lets us assess an opportunity against the same standards each time. For each requirement, record the limit, the reason for it and who needs to verify the supporting evidence.

For example, a purchase budget is only one part of a development brief. The amount available for works, professional fees, finance and contingency also affects whether a project is within reach. Keep these amounts distinct when discussing the search.

Likewise, identify the return measure you want to use. Rental yield, profit on cost and return on cash describe different things. An attractive headline figure needs a clear calculation and stated assumptions before it can help you compare opportunities.

Our deal calculators can help you explore an initial scenario. Record which inputs are estimates and which have supporting evidence, then take the outstanding questions to the relevant professional adviser.

Development needs its own project timetable

For land and commercial-to-residential opportunities, separate the acquisition plan from the plan to deliver the finished project. Buying a building does not establish that the proposed scheme is feasible or that it can be delivered within your budget.

Build the appraisal around the questions that could change the decision: the proposed use, achievable accommodation, condition, access, works costs and evidence for the eventual sale or rental assumptions. Ask your planning, legal, surveying and finance advisers which matters need resolving before you commit.

A practical question for a development opportunity: “Which unresolved assumption could stop this project, who will investigate it, and when will we have an answer?” Give that question a place in the timetable.

Agree a review point for the search

Use the sourcing agreement and consultation to settle how progress will be discussed. At an agreed review point, ask what the evidence says about the brief and which decision is needed next.

If a requirement changes, record the revised instruction. Keeping a dated version of the brief makes it easier to understand why an opportunity was considered and which criteria applied at the time.

Before starting, aim to settle these five points:

  • The intended outcome: the type of asset or project you want to acquire.
  • The boundaries: where the search applies and which requirements are essential.
  • The appraisal basis: the costs, evidence and return measure to use.
  • The responsibilities: what the sourcing service covers and what your advisers will assess.
  • The next review: when to discuss progress and how changes will be agreed.
